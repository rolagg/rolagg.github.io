*Disclaimer: the author is not a lawyer and this post is just a concept proposal for people working in the law field.*

Do you remember the last time you actually read an End User License Agreement for any program you installed? Or maybe you are familiar with GNU GPL? Probably not in much detail. Though it’s actually crucial to understand which rights are given or taken from you when any piece of software is used by any person, whether they are regular or advanced user, especially if the software in question is a proprietary one. So what can we do about it? Let’s digress for a bit.

![](/assets/read-it.jpg "Meanwhile in the parallel universe")

How do you read fiction books? If you read it word by word, thoughtfully enough, chances are you don’t complete more than one in a week. Moreover, you don’t grasp the plot matter much deeper in comparison to how “skimmers” do it. To read more, one shouldn’t pay attention to everything, as the most details will eventually be forgotten by the end of a book. For a programmer, sooner or later it becomes necessary to take a look at the documentation for any somewhat complex tool, and they probably don’t read the whole thing — just the bits that are needed right now. 

I think that the same approach could be applied to legal documents. And I want to make it clear — the way [how Cyberpunk 2077 handled the problem](https://www.cyberpunk.net/en/user-agreement/) is *not* the way to go, as the simplified version of EULA is not the enforced one.

![](/assets/wrong-example.png "How you shouldn't do it")

Now imagine, what if the typical software license would be composed in EPUB format, with each section labelled as a separate chapter for easier navigation; if every OS had a book reader out of the box, which could accept the license file or a link to it on the Internet (if the software product has not been bought yet) as an input? When I opened the GPL 2 in my reader, I was surprised by how short it actually is, as it took 10 pages on my tablet. That’s when I actually read it wholly.

And now take a moment to have another thought — what if EULAs are now built from “blocks”? Every block is a universal legal mini-document, which is created by the community of programmers and lawyers, explaining only one specific aspect of a user's rights and conditions. These blocks would be constantly updated and marked with versions related to adapt to new realities. In some utopian reality, a user could even study these “blocks” in school, so they wouldn’t need to consult a lawyer to fully understand it later in life. 

![](/assets/world-if.jpg "The world if legal documents were accessible to everyone")

While trying to research on the Internet (actually just checking the first pages of Google search) if this topic was already discussed, I couldn’t find any relevant posts regarding the book format or the universal blocks concept. Does this idea have any potential to be utilized in the real world scenario? Or is it too lame because of some legal limitations? 

What do you think?
